## Alternative Investments

This section covers alternative investments, including commodities, real estate, and hedge funds.

### Introduction to Commodities and Commodity Derivatives

This sub-topic introduces commodities and commodity derivatives, focusing on their characteristics, valuation, and the markets they trade in.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Compare characteristics of commodity sectors.**
    Commodity sectors (e.g., energy, agriculture, industrial metals, precious metals) differ in their physical properties, production processes, demand drivers, and storage costs. For instance, energy commodities are often costly to store and transport, while precious metals are relatively easy to store and have monetary as well as industrial uses.
* **Compare the life cycle of commodity sectors from production through trading or consumption.**
    The life cycle of a commodity begins with production (e.g., mining, farming), moves to transportation and storage, and ends with consumption or use in a final product. The length and complexity of this cycle vary by commodity. For example, agricultural commodities have a seasonal production cycle, while metals have a much longer production timeline.
* **Contrast the valuation of commodities with the valuation of equities and bonds.**
    Unlike equities and bonds, which generate cash flows (dividends and interest), most commodities do not. Therefore, their valuation is not based on discounting future cash flows. Instead, commodity valuation is primarily driven by supply and demand dynamics, storage costs, and convenience yield.
* **Describe types of participants in commodity futures markets.**
    Participants include **hedgers** (producers and consumers of the commodity) who use futures to lock in prices and reduce risk, and **speculators** who take on risk in the hope of profiting from price changes. Other participants include **arbitrageurs**, who seek to profit from price discrepancies.
* **Analyze the relationship between spot prices and futures prices in markets in contango and markets in backwardation.**
    In a **contango** market, the futures price is higher than the spot price, which typically occurs when storage costs are high or there is an oversupply of the commodity. In a **backwardation** market, the futures price is lower than the spot price, often due to high demand for the commodity at present (high convenience yield).
* **Compare theories of commodity futures returns.**
    * **Insurance Theory:** Hedgers are willing to pay a premium (in the form of lower expected returns) to speculators for price insurance, leading to a positive expected return for speculators.
    * **Hedging Pressure Hypothesis:** The shape of the futures curve depends on the balance of hedging pressure from producers (who short futures) and consumers (who long futures).
    * **Theory of Storage:** Futures prices are determined by the spot price plus storage costs minus the convenience yield.
* **Describe, calculate, and interpret the components of total return for a fully collateralized commodity futures contract.**
    The total return has three components:
    * **Price Return:** The change in the spot price of the commodity.
    * **Roll Return:** The gain or loss from rolling a maturing futures contract into a new one. A positive roll return occurs in backwardated markets, and a negative roll return occurs in contango markets.
    * **Collateral Return:** The return earned on the collateral posted for the futures position, typically a risk-free rate.
* **Contrast roll return in markets in contango and markets in backwardation.**
    In a **contango** market, a long position in a futures contract will have a negative roll return because the investor sells a cheaper expiring contract and buys a more expensive new contract. In a **backwardation** market, a long position will have a positive roll return because the investor sells an expiring contract at a higher price and buys a new one at a lower price.
* **Describe how commodity swaps are used to obtain or modify exposure to commodities.**
    A commodity swap is an agreement where one party makes payments based on a fixed price and the other makes payments based on a floating price (usually the spot price) of a commodity. Swaps allow investors to gain or hedge exposure to commodity price movements without physically holding the commodity.
* **Describe how the construction of commodity indexes affects index returns.**
    Commodity indexes are weighted averages of the prices of various commodities. The weighting scheme (e.g., based on production, liquidity, or equal weighting) and the roll methodology (how futures contracts are rolled forward) significantly impact the index's performance and risk characteristics.

### Overview of Types of Real Estate Investment

This section provides an overview of real estate investments, including their features, economic drivers, and valuation approaches.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Compare important real estate investment features for valuation purposes.**
    Key features include the property's location, size, age, condition, tenant quality, and lease terms. These factors influence the property's income potential, operating expenses, and overall risk profile.
* **Explain economic value drivers of real estate investments and their role in a portfolio.**
    Economic drivers include population growth, employment trends, interest rates, and inflation. Real estate can provide diversification benefits to a portfolio due to its low correlation with stocks and bonds and can act as an inflation hedge.
* **Discuss the distinctive investment characteristics of commercial property types.**
    Commercial property types include office, retail, industrial, and multifamily. Each has unique characteristics. For example, office properties are sensitive to the business cycle, retail properties depend on consumer spending, industrial properties are influenced by trade and manufacturing, and multifamily properties are driven by demographic trends.
* **Explain the due diligence process and valuation approaches for real estate investments.**
    The due diligence process involves investigating the physical, legal, and financial aspects of a property. Valuation approaches include:
    * **Income Approach:** Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis or direct capitalization of net operating income (NOI).
    * **Sales Comparison Approach:** Comparing the property to similar recently sold properties.
    * **Cost Approach:** Estimating the cost to replace the property.
* **Discuss real estate investment indexes, including their construction and potential biases.**
    Real estate indexes track the performance of a portfolio of properties. They can be appraisal-based or transaction-based. Biases include appraisal smoothing (making returns appear less volatile) and sample selection bias (only properties that are sold are included in transaction-based indexes).

### Investments in Real Estate through Publicly Traded Securities

This sub-topic focuses on real estate investments through publicly traded securities like REITs.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe the types of publicly traded real estate securities.**
    These include **Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)**, which own and operate income-producing real estate, and **Real Estate Operating Companies (REOCs)**, which are similar to REITs but are not required to distribute most of their income to shareholders.
* **Explain the advantages and disadvantages of investing in REITs.**
    **Advantages** include liquidity, diversification, and access to professional management. **Disadvantages** include the potential for stock market volatility to affect REIT prices and the fact that REIT dividends are typically taxed as ordinary income.
* **Describe the valuation of REITs using the net asset value, relative value, and discounted cash flow approaches.**
    * **Net Asset Value (NAV):** Based on the market value of the REIT's underlying properties.
    * **Relative Value:** Using price-to-FFO (Funds From Operations) or price-to-AFFO (Adjusted Funds From Operations) multiples.
    * **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF):** Valuing the REIT based on the present value of its future dividend payments.
* **Calculate and interpret the net asset value per share (NAVPS) of a REIT.**
    NAVPS is calculated by subtracting the REIT's liabilities from the market value of its assets and dividing by the number of shares outstanding. It represents the estimated per-share market value of the REIT's underlying assets.
* **Describe the use of funds from operations (FFO) and adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) in REIT valuation.**
    **FFO** is a measure of a REIT's operating cash flow, calculated as net income plus depreciation minus gains from property sales. **AFFO** is a further refinement of FFO that adjusts for recurring capital expenditures. FFO and AFFO are used in valuation multiples (P/FFO, P/AFFO) and as a basis for estimating a REIT's dividend-paying capacity.
* **Compare the private and public market valuation of real estate.**
    Private market valuation is typically based on appraisals and is less volatile, while public market valuation (of REITs) is based on stock prices and is more volatile. Public market values can trade at a premium or discount to private market values (NAVPS).

### Hedge Fund Strategies

This section details various hedge fund strategies, their characteristics, and their role in a portfolio.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Classify hedge fund strategies.**
    Hedge fund strategies can be broadly classified into four main categories:
    * **Equity Hedge:** Long and short positions in equity securities.
    * **Event-Driven:** Strategies that seek to profit from corporate events such as mergers, acquisitions, and bankruptcies.
    * **Relative Value:** Strategies that seek to profit from pricing discrepancies between related securities.
    * **Opportunistic:** Strategies that take a top-down approach to investing across asset classes.
* **Discuss investment characteristics, strategy implementation, and the role in a portfolio of equity hedge fund strategies.**
    Equity hedge strategies (e.g., long/short equity, market neutral) aim to generate returns from stock selection while managing market risk. They can provide equity-like returns with lower volatility.
* **Discuss investment characteristics, strategy implementation, and the role in a portfolio of event-driven hedge fund strategies.**
    Event-driven strategies (e.g., merger arbitrage, distressed debt) seek to profit from specific corporate events. They tend to have low correlation with traditional asset classes and can provide attractive returns, but they also carry event-specific risks.
* **Discuss investment characteristics, strategy implementation, and the role in a portfolio of relative value hedge fund strategies.**
    Relative value strategies (e.g., convertible arbitrage, fixed-income arbitrage) aim to profit from small price discrepancies between related securities. These strategies typically use high leverage and have low volatility and low correlation with traditional markets.
* **Discuss investment characteristics, strategy implementation, and the role in a portfolio of opportunistic hedge fund strategies.**
    Opportunistic strategies (e.g., global macro, managed futures) take a top-down view of the global economy and invest in various asset classes based on macroeconomic forecasts. They can offer high returns but also have high volatility.
* **Discuss investment characteristics, strategy implementation, and the role in a portfolio of specialist and multi-manager hedge fund strategies.**
    Specialist strategies focus on a particular niche, such as volatility trading. Multi-manager strategies (funds of funds) invest in a portfolio of different hedge funds, offering diversification but also an extra layer of fees.
* **Describe how factor models may be used to understand hedge fund risk exposures.**
    Factor models can be used to identify the underlying risk factors that drive hedge fund returns (e.g., market risk, size, value, momentum). This helps investors understand the sources of risk and return in a hedge fund's strategy and how it might perform in different market environments.

### Analysis of Dividends and Share Repurchases

This section analyzes the different forms of dividends and share repurchases and their impact on shareholder wealth and company value.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe the expected effect of regular cash dividends, extra dividends, liquidating dividends, stock dividends, stock splits, and reverse stock splits on shareholders' wealth and a company's financial ratios.**
    * **Cash Dividends (Regular, Extra, Liquidating):** Reduce cash and equity, decreasing liquidity and solvency ratios. Shareholder wealth is not directly affected, as the stock price theoretically drops by the dividend amount.
    * **Stock Dividends and Stock Splits:** Increase the number of shares outstanding but do not affect the company's assets, liabilities, or equity. Shareholder wealth is not affected. Ratios are affected by the increased number of shares.
    * **Reverse Stock Splits:** Decrease the number of shares outstanding. No effect on shareholder wealth.
* **Compare theories of dividend policy and explain implications of each for share value given a description of a corporate dividend action.**
    * **Dividend Irrelevance Theory (Modigliani-Miller):** In a perfect market, dividend policy is irrelevant to firm value.
    * **Bird-in-Hand Theory (Gordon-Lintner):** Investors prefer dividends because they are less risky than future capital gains. A higher dividend payout increases firm value.
    * **Tax Aversion Theory:** Investors prefer capital gains to dividends because of lower tax rates on capital gains. A lower dividend payout increases firm value.
* **Describe types of information (signals) that dividend initiations, increases, decreases, and omissions may convey.**
    Changes in dividend policy can signal management's view of the company's future prospects. A dividend initiation or increase can signal confidence, while a decrease or omission can signal a negative outlook.
* **Explain how agency costs may affect a company's payout policy.**
    Dividends can reduce agency costs by reducing the amount of free cash flow available to managers, which they might otherwise use for value-destroying projects.
* **Explain factors that affect dividend policy in practice.**
    Factors include investment opportunities, expected volatility of future earnings, financial flexibility, tax considerations, flotation costs, and contractual and legal restrictions.
* **Calculate and interpret the effective tax rate on a given currency unit of corporate earnings under double taxation, dividend imputation, and split-rate tax systems.**
    * **Double Taxation:** Earnings are taxed at the corporate level and again at the individual level when distributed as dividends. Effective tax rate = Corporate tax rate + (1 - Corporate tax rate) * Individual tax rate on dividends.
    * **Dividend Imputation:** Shareholders receive a tax credit for the corporate taxes already paid. The effective tax rate is the shareholder's marginal tax rate.
    * **Split-Rate System:** Different tax rates are applied to retained earnings and distributed earnings.
* **Compare stable dividend with constant dividend payout ratio, and calculate the dividend under each policy.**
    * **Stable Dividend Policy:** Aims to maintain a steady dividend per share, increasing it gradually over time.
    * **Constant Dividend Payout Ratio Policy:** Pays out a constant percentage of earnings as dividends. This leads to a more volatile dividend stream.
* **Describe broad trends in corporate payout policies.**
    In recent decades, there has been a trend towards share repurchases as a way to return cash to shareholders, particularly in the United States.
* **Compare share repurchase methods.**
    * **Open Market Repurchases:** The company buys its own shares on the open market.
    * **Tender Offer:** The company offers to buy back a specific number of shares at a fixed price or within a range of prices.
    * **Direct Negotiation:** The company repurchases shares from a major shareholder.
* **Calculate and compare the effect of a share repurchase on earnings per share when 1) the repurchase is financed with the company's surplus cash and 2) the company uses debt to finance the repurchase.**
    * **Financed with Cash:** EPS will increase if the earnings yield (EPS/Price) is greater than the after-tax yield on the cash used.
    * **Financed with Debt:** EPS will increase if the earnings yield is greater than the after-tax cost of the debt used.
* **Calculate the effect of a share repurchase on book value per share.**
    BVPS will decrease if the stock is repurchased at a price greater than the current BVPS.
* **Explain the choice between paying cash dividends and repurchasing shares.**
    The choice depends on factors such as tax considerations (capital gains vs. dividend income), signaling effects, and flexibility (repurchases are more flexible than dividends).
* **Calculate and interpret dividend coverage ratios based on 1) net income and 2) free cash flow.**
    * **Dividend Payout Ratio:** Dividends / Net Income.
    * **Dividend Coverage Ratio:** Net Income / Dividends.
    * **FCFE Coverage Ratio:** FCFE / (Dividends + Share Repurchases). A higher ratio indicates a greater ability to sustain the dividend.
* **Identify characteristics of companies that may not be able to sustain their cash dividend.**
    Characteristics include high dividend payout ratios, low dividend coverage ratios, volatile earnings, and high levels of debt.

### Cost of Capital: Advanced Topics

This section covers advanced topics related to the cost of capital.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Explain top-down and bottom-up factors that impact the cost of capital.**
    * **Top-down factors** are macroeconomic factors such as interest rates, inflation, and market risk premiums.
    * **Bottom-up factors** are company-specific factors such as industry, size, and leverage.
* **Compare methods used to estimate the cost of debt.**
    * **Yield-to-maturity** on the company's outstanding bonds.
    * **Credit rating approach:** Using the yield on similarly rated bonds.
* **Explain historical and forward-looking approaches to estimating an equity risk premium.**
    * **Historical approach:** Based on the historical average excess return of the equity market over the risk-free rate.
    * **Forward-looking approach:** Based on current market data and expected future returns (e.g., Gordon growth model, Ibbotson-Chen model).
* **Compare methods used to estimate the required return on equity.**
    * **Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM):** Based on the stock's beta and the market risk premium.
    * **Multifactor models:** Such as the Fama-French model, which includes factors for size, value, and other risk factors.
    * **Build-up method:** Used for private companies, starting with the risk-free rate and adding premiums for various risk factors.
* **Estimate the cost of debt or required return on equity for a public company and a private company.**
    For a **public company**, market data can be used to estimate these costs. For a **private company**, comparable public companies are often used to estimate beta and other risk factors.
* **Evaluate a company’s capital structure and cost of capital relative to peers.**
    Comparing a company's capital structure and WACC to its peers can provide insights into its financing decisions and risk profile.

## Portfolio Management

This topic covers portfolio management concepts and strategies.

### Economics and Investment Markets

This section examines the relationship between economics and investment markets.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Explain the notion that to affect market values, economic factors must affect one or more of the following: 1) default-free interest rates across maturities, 2) the timing and/or magnitude of expected cash flows, and 3) risk premiums.**
    Economic factors influence investment values by altering the fundamental inputs to valuation models. Changes in inflation, for instance, affect nominal default-free rates. Economic growth prospects impact the expected future cash flows of companies. Shifts in investor risk aversion, often driven by economic uncertainty, will change the risk premiums demanded for holding risky assets.

* **Explain the role of expectations and changes in expectations in market valuation.**
    Market prices reflect the consensus expectations of all market participants about future economic conditions and company performance. Asset prices change when new information becomes available that causes a revision in these expectations. For example, an unexpected positive economic report can lead to upward revisions in expected earnings and a corresponding increase in stock prices.

* **Explain the relationship between the long-term growth rate of the economy, the volatility of the growth rate, and the average level of real short-term interest rates.**
    A higher long-term trend rate of economic growth is associated with a higher average level of real short-term interest rates. This is because a growing economy creates more investment opportunities, increasing the demand for capital and thus the real cost of borrowing. Conversely, higher volatility in economic growth tends to lower real interest rates, as uncertainty leads to a preference for saving over investment.

* **Explain how the phase of the business cycle affects policy and short-term interest rates, the slope of the term structure of interest rates, and the relative performance of bonds of differing maturities.**
    * **Early Expansion:** Central banks typically maintain low policy rates to support the recovery. The yield curve is usually steep, as investors expect future economic growth and higher inflation, leading to better performance for longer-maturity bonds.
    * **Late Expansion:** To combat rising inflation, central banks begin to raise policy rates. The yield curve tends to flatten as short-term rates rise. Shorter-maturity bonds may outperform.
    * **Contraction/Recession:** Central banks lower policy rates to stimulate the economy. The yield curve steepens as investors anticipate future recovery. Longer-maturity bonds generally perform well due to falling interest rates.

* **Describe the factors that affect yield spreads between non-inflation-adjusted and inflation-indexed bonds.**
    The yield spread between a nominal (non-inflation-adjusted) bond and an inflation-indexed bond of the same maturity reflects the market's expectation of future inflation and a risk premium for inflation uncertainty. A wider spread indicates higher expected inflation and/or a higher inflation risk premium.

* **Explain how the phase of the business cycle affects credit spreads and the performance of credit-sensitive fixed-income instruments.**
    Credit spreads (the difference in yield between corporate bonds and government bonds of similar maturity) tend to narrow during economic expansions as corporate profitability is strong and default risk is low. During recessions, credit spreads widen due to increased default risk. Consequently, credit-sensitive bonds (like corporate bonds) perform best during expansions.

* **Explain how the characteristics of the markets for a company's products affect the company's credit quality.**
    Companies operating in stable, mature industries with low competition and high barriers to entry tend to have higher and more predictable cash flows, leading to better credit quality. Conversely, companies in cyclical or highly competitive industries face more volatile cash flows and thus have lower credit quality.

* **Explain the relationship between the consumption hedging properties of equity and the equity risk premium.**
    The equity risk premium is the excess return that investors demand for holding equities over a risk-free asset. If equities provide a good hedge against unexpected changes in consumption (i.e., if equity returns are high when consumption is unexpectedly low), the equity risk premium will be lower. However, empirical evidence suggests that equity is a poor hedge against consumption risk, which helps to explain why the equity risk premium is positive.

* **Explain how the phase of the business cycle affects short-term and long-term earnings growth expectations.**
    During an economic expansion, both short-term and long-term earnings growth expectations are typically revised upwards. In a recession, these expectations are revised downwards. The impact is generally more pronounced for short-term expectations.

* **Describe cyclical effects on valuation multiples.**
    Valuation multiples, such as the P/E ratio, tend to expand during economic expansions and contract during recessions. This is because investor sentiment is more optimistic during expansions, leading to higher stock prices relative to earnings.

* **Describe the economic factors affecting investment in commercial real estate.**
    The demand for commercial real estate is driven by economic growth, employment levels, and consumer spending. Interest rates affect the cost of financing for real estate projects, and inflation can impact both rental income and property values.

**Equations:**

* **Present Value Model:**
    $P_t^i = \sum_{s=1}^{N} \frac{E_t[\overline{CF}_{t+s}^i]}{(1 + l_{t,s} + \theta_{t,s} + \rho_{t,s}^i)^s}$
    Where:
    $P_t^i$ = price of asset i at time t
    $E_t[\overline{CF}_{t+s}^i]$ = expected cash flow of asset i at time t+s
    $l_{t,s}$ = risk-free rate for a maturity of s at time t
    $\theta_{t,s}$ = risk premium for time s
    $\rho_{t,s}^i$ = asset-specific risk premium for asset i at time t for a maturity of s

* **Taylor Rule:**
    $pr_t = l_t + \iota_t + 0.5(\iota_t - \iota_t^*) + 0.5(Y_t - Y_t^*)$
    Where:
    $pr_t$ = policy rate
    $l_t$ = real interest rate
    $\iota_t$ = inflation rate
    $\iota_t^*$ = target inflation rate
    $Y_t$ = actual output
    $Y_t^*$ = potential output

* **Expected Loss:**
    *Expected loss = Probability of default × (1 - Recovery rate)*

### Analysis of Active Portfolio Management

This sub-topic covers the analysis of active portfolio management.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe how value added by active management is measured.**
    Value added, also known as the active return or alpha, is the difference between the portfolio's return and the benchmark's return: $R_A = R_P - R_B$.

* **Calculate and interpret the information ratio (ex post and ex ante) and contrast it to the Sharpe ratio.**
    The **Information Ratio (IR)** measures the active return per unit of active risk. A higher IR indicates better risk-adjusted active performance.
    * **Ex post IR** is calculated using historical data.
    * **Ex ante IR** is a forward-looking estimate.
    The **Sharpe Ratio** measures the portfolio's excess return (over the risk-free rate) per unit of total risk. The IR focuses on active management skill, while the Sharpe ratio assesses overall portfolio performance.

* **Describe and interpret the fundamental law of active portfolio management, including its component terms-transfer coefficient, information coefficient, breadth, and active risk (aggressiveness).**
    The fundamental law states that the expected information ratio depends on the manager's skill (Information Coefficient, IC) and the number of independent investment decisions made (Breadth, BR).
    * **Information Coefficient (IC):** The correlation between the manager's forecasts and actual outcomes.
    * **Breadth (BR):** The number of independent active bets taken per year.
    * **Transfer Coefficient (TC):** A measure of the constraints on the manager's ability to implement their desired active weights. A TC of 1 means no constraints.
    * **Active Risk:** The standard deviation of the active return, representing the manager's aggressiveness.

* **Explain how the information ratio may be useful in investment manager selection and choosing the level of active portfolio risk.**
    The IR helps in selecting managers who have demonstrated skill in generating excess returns. The optimal level of active risk for a portfolio can be determined based on the investor's risk tolerance and the manager's IR.

* **Compare active management strategies, including market timing and security selection, and evaluate strategy changes in terms of the fundamental law of active management.**
    * **Security Selection:** Involves forecasting the returns of individual securities (high breadth).
    * **Market Timing:** Involves forecasting the returns of the overall market or asset classes (low breadth).
    The fundamental law suggests that for a given level of skill (IC), strategies with higher breadth have the potential for a higher IR.

* **Describe the practical strengths and limitations of the fundamental law of active management.**
    **Strengths:** Provides a useful framework for understanding the sources of active management performance.
    **Limitations:** It can be difficult to accurately measure IC and BR, and the assumption of independent investment decisions may not always hold.

**Equations:**

* **Information Ratio (IR):**
    $IR = \frac{R_P - R_B}{\sigma(R_P - R_B)}$

* **Sharpe Ratio of Actively Managed Portfolio:**
    $SR_P^2 = SR_B^2 + IR^2$

* **Optimal Level of Active Risk:**
    $\sigma(R_A) = \frac{IR}{SR_B}\sigma_B$

* **Basic Fundamental Law:**
    $E(R_A)^* = IC \sqrt{BR} \sigma_A$

* **Expanded Fundamental Law:**
    $E(R_A) = (TC)(IC)\sqrt{BR}\sigma_A$

### Exchange-Traded Funds: Mechanics and Applications

This section covers the mechanics and applications of ETFs.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe the mechanics of creating and redeeming ETF shares.**
    Authorized participants (APs) create new ETF shares by delivering a basket of the underlying securities to the ETF sponsor. They redeem ETF shares by exchanging them for the underlying securities. This creation/redemption mechanism helps to keep the ETF's market price close to its net asset value (NAV).

* **Describe trading and settlement of ETF shares.**
    ETF shares are traded on stock exchanges throughout the day, just like individual stocks. Settlement for ETF trades is typically T+2 (trade date plus two business days).

* **Describe the costs of ETF ownership.**
    Costs include the expense ratio (annual management fee), trading commissions, and the bid-ask spread.

* **Describe ETF risks.**
    Risks include market risk (the risk of the underlying assets), tracking error (the difference between the ETF's return and the index's return), and counterparty risk (in the case of synthetic ETFs).

* **Describe ETF strategies and their use in portfolio management.**
    ETFs can be used for various strategies, including asset allocation, sector rotation, hedging, and gaining exposure to specific market segments or factors.

### Using Multifactor Models

This section discusses the use of multifactor models in portfolio management.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe arbitrage pricing theory (APT), including its underlying assumptions and its relation to multifactor models.**
    **APT** is an asset pricing model that suggests an asset's expected return is a linear function of its sensitivities to various systematic risk factors.
    **Assumptions:**
    1.  Asset returns can be described by a factor model.
    2.  There are no arbitrage opportunities.
    3.  Asset-specific risk can be diversified away.
    Multifactor models are the practical implementation of APT.

* **Define arbitrage opportunity and determine whether an arbitrage opportunity exists.**
    An arbitrage opportunity is a risk-free profit that can be earned with zero investment. In the context of APT, an arbitrage opportunity exists if two portfolios with the same factor exposures have different expected returns.

* **Calculate the expected return on an asset given an asset’s factor sensitivities and the factor risk premiums.**
    The expected return is calculated by multiplying each factor sensitivity (beta) by its corresponding factor risk premium and adding the risk-free rate.

* **Describe and compare macroeconomic factor models, fundamental factor models, and statistical factor models.**
    * **Macroeconomic factor models:** Use observable economic variables (e.g., inflation, GDP growth) as factors.
    * **Fundamental factor models:** Use company-specific characteristics (e.g., P/E ratio, market cap) as factors.
    * **Statistical factor models:** Use statistical methods (e.g., principal component analysis) to extract unobservable factors from historical return data.

* **Describe uses of multifactor models and interpret the output of analyses based on multifactor models.**
    Multifactor models are used for return attribution, risk analysis, and portfolio construction. The output of a multifactor model analysis can show how much of a portfolio's return is due to its exposure to different risk factors.

* **Describe the potential benefits for investors in considering multiple risk dimensions when modeling asset returns.**
    Considering multiple risk dimensions provides a more complete picture of a portfolio's risk exposures than a single-factor model (like the CAPM). This can lead to better risk management and portfolio construction decisions.

* **Explain sources of active risk and interpret tracking risk and the information ratio.**
    **Sources of active risk** include active factor tilts (having different factor exposures than the benchmark) and active security selection.
    **Tracking risk** (or active risk) is the standard deviation of the active return.
    The **information ratio** measures the active return per unit of tracking risk.

**Equations:**

* **Multifactor Model:**
    $R_i = a_i + b_{i1}I_1 + b_{i2}I_2 + ... + b_{iK}I_K + \epsilon_i$

* **APT:**
    $E(R_p) = R_F + \lambda_1\beta_{p,1} + ... + \lambda_K\beta_{p,K}$

* **Carhart Four-Factor Model:**
    $R_p - R_F = a_p + b_{p1}RMRF + b_{p2}SMB + b_{p3}HML + b_{p4}WML + \epsilon_p$

### Measuring and Managing Market Risk

This section covers the measurement and management of market risk.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe and interpret value at risk (VaR).**
    **VaR** is a measure of the maximum potential loss on a portfolio over a given time horizon at a given confidence level. For example, a 1-day 95% VaR of $1 million means that there is a 5% chance of losing more than $1 million on any given day.

* **Compare the parametric (variance–covariance), historical simulation, and Monte Carlo simulation methods for estimating VaR.**
    * **Parametric method:** Assumes that portfolio returns are normally distributed and uses historical mean and standard deviation to estimate VaR.
    * **Historical simulation:** Uses historical price data to simulate potential future returns and does not assume a specific distribution.
    * **Monte Carlo simulation:** Uses a specified probability distribution and random number generation to simulate a large number of possible future price paths.

* **Estimate and interpret VaR under the parametric, historical simulation, and Monte Carlo simulation methods.**
    Each method will produce a VaR estimate. The parametric method is the simplest but relies on the assumption of normality. Historical simulation is non-parametric but is limited by the available historical data. Monte Carlo simulation is the most flexible but requires assumptions about the return distribution.

* **Describe sensitivity risk measures and scenario risk measures and compare these measures to VaR.**
    * **Sensitivity measures** (e.g., beta, duration) measure the change in a portfolio's value for a given change in a risk factor.
    * **Scenario analysis** (or stress testing) examines the impact of extreme market events on a portfolio's value.
    These measures provide different perspectives on risk than VaR. VaR provides a single number for potential loss, while sensitivity and scenario analysis provide more detail about how a portfolio's value will change under specific conditions.

* **Describe the use of constraints in managing market risk.**
    Constraints such as position limits, leverage limits, and sector exposure limits can be used to manage market risk by preventing excessive risk-taking.

* **Explain how risk measures are used in capital allocation decisions.**
    Risk measures are used to determine the amount of capital that should be allocated to different business units or investment strategies based on their risk-adjusted return potential.

* **Describe the different risk measures used by banks, asset managers, pension funds, and insurers.**
    Different types of financial institutions use different risk measures depending on their business model and regulatory requirements. For example, banks are heavily focused on VaR and stress testing for regulatory capital purposes, while asset managers may use a wider range of risk measures to manage their portfolios.

### Backtesting and Simulation

This section discusses backtesting and simulation of investment strategies.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe objectives in backtesting an investment strategy.**
    The main objective of backtesting is to evaluate the historical performance of a trading strategy to see how it would have performed in the past. This helps to assess the strategy's potential for future profitability.

* **Describe and contrast steps and procedures in backtesting an investment strategy.**
    Steps include defining the strategy rules, obtaining historical data, simulating the strategy's trades, and evaluating the performance. It is important to account for realistic trading costs and to avoid look-ahead bias (using information that would not have been available at the time of the trade).

* **Interpret metrics and visuals reported in a backtest of an investment strategy.**
    Metrics include total return, annualized return, standard deviation, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown. Visuals can include an equity curve chart showing the strategy's cumulative return over time.

* **Identify problems in a backtest of an investment strategy.**
    Problems can include data snooping (over-optimizing the strategy on the historical data), survivorship bias (excluding failed companies from the data), and unrealistic assumptions about trading costs and liquidity.

* **Evaluate and interpret a historical scenario analysis.**
    A historical scenario analysis examines how a portfolio would have performed during a specific historical market event (e.g., the 2008 financial crisis). This can provide insights into the portfolio's tail risk.

* **Contrast Monte Carlo and historical simulation approaches.**
    **Monte Carlo simulation** uses a specified probability distribution to generate random scenarios, while **historical simulation** uses actual historical data to create scenarios. Monte Carlo simulation is more flexible but requires assumptions, while historical simulation is limited by the available data.

* **Explain inputs and decisions in simulation and interpret a simulation.**
    Inputs for a simulation include assumptions about asset returns, volatilities, and correlations. Decisions include the choice of simulation method and the number of simulations to run. The output of a simulation can be used to estimate a portfolio's expected return, risk, and distribution of potential outcomes.

* **Demonstrate the use of sensitivity analysis.**
    Sensitivity analysis involves changing one or more input assumptions in a model to see how it affects the output. This can help to understand the model's key drivers and its robustness to different assumptions.

## Derivatives

This section provides an in-depth look at derivatives, covering both forward commitments and contingent claims.

### Pricing and Valuation of Forward Commitments

This sub-topic focuses on the pricing and valuation of forward commitments such as forwards, futures, and swaps.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe how equity forwards and futures are priced, and calculate and interpret their no-arbitrage value.**
    The no-arbitrage price of an equity forward or future is the spot price of the underlying asset compounded at the risk-free rate, less the present value of any dividends to be paid during the life of the contract.

* **Describe the carry arbitrage model without underlying cashflows and with underlying cashflows.**
    The carry arbitrage model states that the forward price must be equal to the spot price plus the cost of carry (financing costs minus any income earned on the asset). If the forward price is different, an arbitrage opportunity exists.

* **Describe how interest rate forwards and futures are priced, and calculate and interpret their no-arbitrage value.**
    The price of an interest rate forward (Forward Rate Agreement, FRA) or future is based on the implied forward interest rate derived from the current spot yield curve.

* **Describe how fixed-income forwards and futures are priced, and calculate and interpret their no-arbitrage value.**
    The price of a fixed-income forward or future is the spot price of the underlying bond compounded at the risk-free rate, less the present value of any coupon payments.

* **Describe how interest rate swaps are priced, and calculate and interpret their no-arbitrage value.**
    An interest rate swap is priced such that the present value of the fixed-rate payments is equal to the present value of the expected floating-rate payments at the initiation of the swap. The fixed rate that makes these two present values equal is the swap rate.

* **Describe how currency swaps are priced, and calculate and interpret their no-arbitrage value.**
    A currency swap involves the exchange of principal and interest payments in different currencies. It is priced such that the present value of the payments in one currency is equal to the present value of the payments in the other currency, using the current exchange rate.

* **Describe how equity swaps are priced, and calculate and interpret their no-arbitrage value.**
    In an equity swap, one party pays the return on an equity index and the other pays a fixed or floating interest rate. The swap is priced so that the present value of the expected equity return payments is equal to the present value of the fixed or floating rate payments.

**Equations:**

* **Forward Price (No underlying cash flows):**
    $F_0 = FV(S_0)$

* **Value of Forward Contract (Long):**
    $V_t = PV[F_t - F_0]$ or $V_t = S_t - PV[F_0]$

* **Forward Price (With underlying cash flows):**
    $F_0 = FV[S_0 + CC_0 - CB_0]$

* **Swap Rate (Fixed):**
    $r_{FIX} = \frac{1 - PV_n(1)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}PV_i(1)}$

* **Value of Swap (Receive-Fixed):**
    $V_{swap} = V_{FIX} - V_{FLT}$

* **Upfront Payment (CDS):**
    *Upfront payment = PV(Protection leg) – PV(Premium leg)*

* **Value of Equity Swap:**
    $V_{EQ,t} = V_{FIX}(C_0) - (\frac{S_t}{S_{t-1}})NA_E - PV(Par - NA_E)$

### Valuation of Contingent Claims

This section covers the valuation of contingent claims, primarily options.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe and interpret the binomial option valuation model and its component terms.**
    The binomial model is a discrete-time model that values an option by creating a risk-neutral portfolio of the option and the underlying asset. The model assumes that the price of the underlying asset can only move to one of two possible prices in the next time period.

* **Describe how the value of a European option can be analyzed as the present value of the option's expected payoff at expiration.**
    The value of a European option is the present value of its expected payoff at expiration, discounted at the risk-free rate, using risk-neutral probabilities.

* **Identify an arbitrage opportunity involving options and describe the related arbitrage.**
    An arbitrage opportunity exists if the put-call parity relationship is violated. For example, if the actual price of a call option is higher than its theoretical price based on put-call parity, an arbitrageur can sell the call, buy the put, sell short the stock, and lend at the risk-free rate to lock in a risk-free profit.

* **Calculate the no-arbitrage values of European and American options using a two-period binomial model.**
    The binomial model can be extended to multiple periods. The value of the option at each node is calculated by working backward from the expiration date. For an American option, the value at each node is the greater of the value if exercised immediately and the value if held for another period.

* **Calculate and interpret the value of an interest rate option using a two-period binomial model.**
    A binomial model can also be used to value interest rate options by modeling the evolution of the short-term interest rate over time.

* **Identify assumptions of the Black-Scholes-Merton option valuation model.**
    **Assumptions:**
    1.  The price of the underlying asset follows a lognormal distribution.
    2.  The risk-free rate and volatility are constant.
    3.  There are no transaction costs or taxes.
    4.  The underlying asset pays no dividends.
    5.  The option is European.

* **Interpret the components of the Black-Scholes-Merton model as applied to call options in terms of a leveraged position in the underlying.**
    The BSM formula can be interpreted as the value of a leveraged position in the underlying asset. The term $SN(d_1)$ represents the value of buying a fraction of a share of the stock, and the term $-e^{-rT}XN(d_2)$ represents the value of borrowing at the risk-free rate.

* **Describe how the Black-Scholes-Merton model is used to value European options on equities and currencies.**
    The BSM model can be adapted to value options on different types of underlying assets by adjusting for dividends (for equities) or the foreign risk-free rate (for currencies).

* **Describe how the Black model is used to value European options on futures.**
    The Black model is a variation of the BSM model that is used to value options on futures contracts. The main difference is that the spot price of the underlying asset is replaced by the futures price.

* **Describe how the Black model is used to value European interest rate options and European swaptions.**
    The Black model can also be used to value interest rate options (caps and floors) and swaptions (options to enter into an interest rate swap).

* **Interpret each of the option Greeks.**
    The "Greeks" are measures of an option's sensitivity to different risk factors:
    * **Delta:** Sensitivity to the price of the underlying asset.
    * **Gamma:** Sensitivity of delta to the price of the underlying asset.
    * **Vega:** Sensitivity to the volatility of the underlying asset.
    * **Theta:** Sensitivity to the passage of time.
    * **Rho:** Sensitivity to the risk-free interest rate.

* **Describe how a delta hedge is executed.**
    A delta hedge is a strategy used to reduce the directional risk of an option position by taking an offsetting position in the underlying asset. For example, to delta-hedge a long call option position, an investor would sell short a number of shares of the underlying stock equal to the option's delta.

* **Describe the role of gamma risk in options trading.**
    Gamma risk is the risk that an option's delta will change as the price of the underlying asset changes. This means that a delta-hedged position will need to be rebalanced frequently to maintain its delta neutrality.

* **Define implied volatility and explain how it is used in options trading.**
    **Implied volatility** is the volatility that is implied by the current market price of an option. It is the value of volatility that, when plugged into the BSM model, produces the option's market price. Traders use implied volatility to assess whether an option is cheap or expensive relative to their own forecast of future volatility.

**Equations:**

* **Hedge Ratio (h):**
    $h = \frac{c^+ - c^-}{S^+ - S^-}$

* **No-arbitrage single-period call option valuation:**
    $c = hS + PV(-hS^- + c^-)$

* **No-arbitrage single-period put option valuation:**
    $p = hS + PV(-hS^- + p^-)$

* **Put-call parity:**
    $S + p = PV(X) + c$

* **Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) Model (Call):**
    $c = SN(d_1) - e^{-rT}XN(d_2)$
    where $d_1 = \frac{ln(S/X) + (r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2})T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$ and $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$

* **Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) Model (Put):**
    $p = e^{-rT}XN(-d_2) - SN(-d_1)$

## Equity Valuation

This topic focuses on the valuation of equity securities, which involves determining the intrinsic value of a company's stock.

### Equity Valuation: Applications and Processes

This section introduces the fundamental concepts, applications, and processes involved in valuing a company's equity.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Define valuation and intrinsic value and explain sources of perceived mispricing.**
    * **Valuation** is the process of estimating the worth of an asset.
    * **Intrinsic value** is the true, underlying value of an asset based on a comprehensive analysis of its fundamentals (cash flows, growth, and risk).
    * **Perceived mispricing** arises when the market price of a security deviates from its estimated intrinsic value. This can happen due to inefficient markets, incorrect analysis by market participants, or behavioral biases.

* **Explain the going concern assumption and contrast a going concern value to a liquidation value.**
    * The **going concern assumption** presumes that a company will continue to operate indefinitely. **Going concern value** is the valuation of a company under this assumption, typically based on its expected future cash flows.
    * **Liquidation value** is the estimated amount of money a company would realize if it were to sell all its assets and pay off all its liabilities. This value is relevant for companies facing financial distress or bankruptcy.

* **Describe definitions of value and justify which definition of value is most relevant to public company valuation.**
    * **Intrinsic Value:** The most relevant for public company valuation, as it reflects the company's long-term cash-generating ability.
    * **Fair Market Value:** The price at which an asset would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts.
    * **Investment Value:** The value of an asset to a particular investor, based on their individual investment requirements and expectations.

* **Describe applications of equity valuation.**
    Valuation is used in various contexts, including:
    * **Stock Selection:** Identifying overvalued or undervalued stocks.
    * **Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A):** Determining the purchase price of a target company.
    * **Initial Public Offerings (IPOs):** Setting the initial price for a company's stock.
    * **Corporate Finance:** Evaluating investment projects and strategic decisions.
    * **Litigation:** Assessing damages in legal disputes.

* **Describe questions that should be addressed in conducting an industry and competitive analysis.**
    An analyst should ask:
    * What are the key economic and technological drivers of the industry?
    * What is the industry's structure (e.g., fragmented, consolidated)?
    * What is the level of competition? (Porter's Five Forces is a useful framework).
    * What is the company's competitive strategy and position within the industry?

* **Contrast absolute and relative valuation models and describe examples of each type of model.**
    * **Absolute valuation models** determine an asset's intrinsic value without reference to other assets. Examples include the **Discounted Dividend Model (DDM)** and the **Free Cash Flow (FCF) model**.
    * **Relative valuation models** value an asset by comparing it to similar assets. Examples include using **price-to-earnings (P/E)** or **price-to-book (P/B)** multiples.

* **Describe sum-of-the-parts valuation and conglomerate discounts.**
    * **Sum-of-the-parts valuation** involves valuing each division or segment of a company separately and then adding them up to get the total value of the company.
    * A **conglomerate discount** occurs when the market values a diversified company at less than the sum of the individual values of its parts. This can be due to inefficiencies in capital allocation or a lack of synergy between the different businesses.

* **Explain broad criteria for choosing an appropriate approach for valuing a given company.**
    The choice of valuation model depends on the characteristics of the company being valued. For example, a DDM is suitable for a stable, mature company that pays dividends, while a FCF model is more appropriate for a company that does not pay dividends or has volatile capital expenditures.

**Equations:**

* **Perceived Mispricing:**
    $V_E - P = (V - P) + (V_E - V)$
    Where:
    * $V_E$ = Estimated value
    * $P$ = Market price
    * $V$ = Intrinsic value

### Discounted Dividend Valuation

This section covers the dividend discount model (DDM) and its variations, which value a stock based on the present value of its future dividend payments.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Compare dividends, free cash flow, and residual income as inputs to discounted cash flow models and identify investment situations for which each measure is suitable.**
    * **Dividends:** Suitable for mature, dividend-paying companies.
    * **Free Cash Flow (FCF):** Suitable for companies that do not pay dividends or have unpredictable dividend patterns.
    * **Residual Income:** Suitable for companies with negative free cash flow, as it focuses on accounting profitability.

* **Calculate and interpret the value of a common stock using the dividend discount model (DDM) for single and multiple holding periods.**
    The value of a stock is the present value of its expected future dividends plus the present value of its expected future selling price.

* **Calculate the value of a common stock using the Gordon growth model and explain the model's underlying assumptions.**
    The Gordon growth model values a stock assuming that dividends grow at a constant rate indefinitely.
    **Assumptions:**
    1.  Dividends grow at a constant rate, *g*.
    2.  The required rate of return, *r*, is greater than the growth rate, *g*.

* **Calculate the value of non-callable fixed-rate perpetual preferred stock.**
    Perpetual preferred stock pays a fixed dividend forever. Its value is calculated by dividing the annual dividend by the required rate of return.

* **Describe strengths and limitations of the Gordon growth model and justify its selection to value a company's common shares.**
    * **Strengths:** Simple and easy to use.
    * **Limitations:** Sensitive to the inputs for *r* and *g*, and the assumption of constant growth is often unrealistic.
    It is most suitable for valuing stable, mature companies in non-cyclical industries.

* **Calculate and interpret the implied growth rate of dividends using the Gordon growth model and current stock price.**
    The Gordon growth model can be rearranged to solve for the implied growth rate of dividends that is priced into the current stock price.

* **Calculate and interpret the present value of growth opportunities (PVGO) and the component of the leading price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) related to PVGO.**
    **PVGO** represents the portion of a stock's value that comes from the company's ability to reinvest earnings at a rate of return greater than the required rate of return. A higher PVGO leads to a higher P/E ratio.

* **Calculate and interpret the justified leading and trailing P/Es using the Gordon growth model.**
    The "justified" P/E is the P/E ratio that is consistent with the company's fundamentals (payout ratio, growth rate, and required return) according to the Gordon growth model.

* **Estimate a required return based on any DDM, including the Gordon growth model and the H-model.**
    The DDM can be rearranged to solve for the implied required rate of return, *r*.

* **Evaluate whether a stock is overvalued, fairly valued, or undervalued by the market based on a DDM estimate of value.**
    If the DDM-estimated value is greater than the market price, the stock is undervalued. If it is less, the stock is overvalued.

* **Explain the growth phase, transition phase, and maturity phase of a business.**
    A company's life cycle can be divided into three phases:
    1.  **Growth Phase:** Rapid growth in sales and earnings.
    2.  **Transition Phase:** Growth slows down as the company matures.
    3.  **Maturity Phase:** Stable growth at a rate close to the long-term economic growth rate.

* **Explain the assumptions and justify the selection of the two-stage DDM, the H-model, the three-stage DDM, or spreadsheet modeling to value a company's common shares.**
    These multistage models are used for companies that are not expected to have constant growth.
    * **Two-stage DDM:** Assumes a period of high growth followed by a period of stable growth.
    * **H-model:** Assumes that the growth rate declines linearly over a specified period.
    * **Three-stage DDM:** Includes a growth, transition, and maturity phase.
    * **Spreadsheet modeling:** Provides the most flexibility for forecasting dividends.

* **Describe terminal value and explain alternative approaches to determining the terminal value in a DDM.**
    **Terminal value** is the estimated value of the stock at the end of the high-growth period. It can be calculated using the Gordon growth model or by using a price multiple.

* **Calculate and interpret the value of common shares using the two-stage DDM, the H-model, and the three-stage DDM.**
    These models involve discounting the dividends during the high-growth and transition periods and then adding the present value of the terminal value.

* **Explain the use of spreadsheet modeling to forecast dividends and to value common shares.**
    Spreadsheet models allow for detailed, year-by-year forecasting of a company's financial statements, from which dividends can be derived and discounted.

* **Calculate and interpret the sustainable growth rate of a company and demonstrate the use of DuPont analysis to estimate a company's sustainable growth rate.**
    The **sustainable growth rate (SGR)** is the rate at which a company can grow its earnings and dividends without changing its financing policy. It is calculated as the retention ratio multiplied by the return on equity (ROE). DuPont analysis breaks down ROE into its components (profitability, asset efficiency, and financial leverage) to better understand the drivers of the SGR.

**Equations:**

* **DDM (single holding period):**
    $V_0 = \frac{D_1 + P_1}{1 + r}$

* **General DDM:**
    $V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{D_t}{(1 + r)^t}$

* **Gordon Growth Model:**
    $V_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g}$

* **Value of Perpetual Preferred Stock:**
    $V_0 = \frac{D}{r}$

* **PVGO:**
    $V_0 = \frac{E_1}{r} + PVGO$

* **Justified Leading P/E:**
    $\frac{P_0}{E_1} = \frac{1 - b}{r - g}$

* **Justified Trailing P/E:**
    $\frac{P_0}{E_0} = \frac{(1 - b)(1 + g)}{r - g}$

* **Sustainable Growth Rate (g):**
    $g = b \times ROE$

### Free Cash Flow Valuation

This sub-topic covers valuation using free cash flow models, which are particularly useful for companies that don't pay dividends.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Compare the free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) and free cash flow to equity (FCFE) approaches to valuation.**
    * **FCFF** is the cash flow available to all of the company's capital providers (both debt and equity holders). It is discounted at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) to get the value of the firm.
    * **FCFE** is the cash flow available to equity holders only. It is discounted at the cost of equity to get the value of equity.

* **Calculate FCFF and FCFE.**
    FCFF and FCFE can be calculated starting from net income, cash flow from operations, EBIT, or EBITDA. The key is to adjust for non-cash charges and investments in working capital and fixed capital.

* **Describe approaches for forecasting FCFF and FCFE.**
    FCFF and FCFE can be forecasted by forecasting the components of the cash flow statements, often as a percentage of sales.

* **Explain the single-stage (stable-growth), two-stage, and three-stage FCFF and FCFE models and justify the selection of the appropriate model given a company’s characteristics.**
    Similar to the DDM, free cash flow models can be used in single-stage or multistage formats, depending on the company's expected growth pattern.

* **Estimate a company’s value using the appropriate free cash flow model(s).**
    This involves forecasting future free cash flows, determining the appropriate discount rate, and calculating the present value of the cash flows.

* **Describe approaches for calculating the terminal value in a multistage valuation model.**
    The terminal value in a free cash flow model is typically calculated using a constant-growth model or an exit multiple approach.

**Equations:**

* **FCFF from NI:**
    *FCFF = NI + NCC + Int(1 – Tax rate) – FCInv – WCInv*
* **FCFE from NI:**
    *FCFE = NI + NCC – FCInv – WCInv + Net borrowing*
* **FCFF from CFO:**
    *FCFF = CFO + Int(1 – Tax rate) – FCInv*
* **FCFE from CFO:**
    *FCFE = CFO – FCInv + Net borrowing*
* **FCFE from FCFF:**
    *FCFE = FCFF – Int(1 – Tax rate) + Net borrowing*
* **FCFF from EBIT:**
    *FCFF = EBIT(1 – Tax rate) + Dep – FCInv – WCInv*
* **FCFF from EBITDA:**
    *FCFF = EBITDA(1 – Tax rate) + Dep(Tax rate) – FCInv – WCInv*
* **Firm Value (single-stage FCFF):**
    *Firm value = $\frac{FCFF_1}{WACC - g}$*
* **Equity Value (single-stage FCFE):**
    *Equity value = $\frac{FCFE_1}{r - g}$*

### Market-Based Valuation: Price and Enterprise Value Multiples

This sub-topic covers valuation using market-based multiples, a form of relative valuation.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe and interpret price and enterprise value multiples and their use in valuation.**
    * **Price multiples** (e.g., P/E, P/B, P/S, P/CF) relate the stock price to a measure of value per share.
    * **Enterprise value multiples** (e.g., EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales) relate the total value of the company to a measure of value for the entire firm.
    These multiples are used to compare a company to its peers or to its own historical valuation.

* **Describe fundamental factors that influence alternative price multiples and dividend yield.**
    The fundamental factors that drive price multiples are the required rate of return and the expected growth rate. A higher growth rate and a lower required return will lead to higher multiples.

* **Calculate and interpret a predicted P/E, given a cross-sectional regression on fundamentals, and explain limitations to the cross-sectional regression methodology.**

The model can then be used to predict the P/E for a specific company. Limitations include the potential for omitted variable bias and the assumption of a linear relationship.

* **Explain sources of differences in cross-border valuation comparisons.**
    Differences in accounting standards, economic conditions, and cultural factors can make it difficult to compare valuation multiples across different countries.

### Residual Income Valuation

This section focuses on the residual income valuation model, an absolute valuation model based on accounting data.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe and calculate residual income.**
    **Residual income** is the net income of a company less a charge for the cost of equity capital. It represents the economic profit of the company.

* **Explain the residual income model and its relation to other valuation models.**
    The residual income model values a company as the sum of its current book value of equity and the present value of its expected future residual income. It is mathematically equivalent to the DDM and the FCFE model, assuming consistent assumptions.

* **Calculate the value of a stock using the residual income model.**
    This involves forecasting future residual income and discounting it back to the present.

* **Describe the strengths and weaknesses of the residual income model.**
    * **Strengths:** Uses readily available accounting data and is less sensitive to terminal value estimates than other models.
    * **Weaknesses:** Relies on accounting data, which can be subject to manipulation, and requires detailed forecasts.

* **Explain accounting and international considerations in using the residual income model.**
    Adjustments may be needed for differences in accounting standards (e.g., IFRS vs. US GAAP) and for factors such as violations of the clean surplus relationship.

**Equations:**

* **Residual Income (RI):**
    $RI_t = E_t - rB_{t-1}$
* **Residual Income Model:**
    $V_0 = B_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{RI_t}{(1 + r)^t}$

### Private Company Valuation

This sub-topic discusses the unique challenges and approaches to valuing private companies.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Compare and contrast public and private company valuation.**
    Valuing private companies is more challenging due to the lack of publicly available data and the illiquidity of the shares. Adjustments for control and lack of marketability are often required.

* **Describe the uses of private company valuation.**
    Valuation of private companies is needed for transactions (M&A, IPOs), tax purposes (estate and gift taxes), and litigation.

* **Explain earnings normalization and cash flow estimation issues for private companies.**
    Adjustments may be needed to normalize earnings for non-recurring items, related-party transactions, and differences in accounting methods.

* **Describe approaches for estimating the required rate of return for private companies.**
    The required rate of return for a private company is typically higher than for a similar public company to compensate for the additional risk and lack of liquidity. The build-up method is often used to estimate the cost of equity.

* **Explain valuation discounts and premiums.**
    * **Discount for Lack of Control (DLOC):** Applied to minority interests in private companies.
    * **Discount for Lack of Marketability (DLOM):** Applied to reflect the illiquidity of private company shares.
    * **Control Premium:** The additional value that an acquirer is willing to pay to gain control of a company.

* **Describe income-based, market-based, and asset-based approaches to private company valuation.**
    * **Income-based:** DCF models (FCFF or FCFE).
    * **Market-based:** Using multiples from comparable public companies or from recent transactions of similar private companies.
    * **Asset-based:** Valuing the company based on the fair market value of its assets less its liabilities.

### The Term Structure and Interest Rate Dynamics

This section discusses the relationship between interest rates and time to maturity, known as the term structure of interest rates.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe relationships among spot rates, forward rates, yield to maturity, expected and realized returns on bonds, and the shape of the yield curve.**
    * **Spot rates** are the yields on zero-coupon bonds. The **yield curve** is a plot of spot rates against maturity.
    * **Forward rates** are interest rates for a future period that are implied by the current spot curve.
    * The **yield to maturity (YTM)** is the single discount rate that equates the present value of a bond's cash flows to its market price. It is a weighted average of the spot rates.
    * The expected return on a bond will equal its YTM only if the yield curve is flat and does not change.

* **Describe how zero-coupon rates (spot rates) may be obtained from the par curve by bootstrapping.**
    **Bootstrapping** is a process of sequentially calculating spot rates for different maturities using the prices of coupon-paying bonds.

* **Describe the assumptions concerning the evolution of spot rates in relation to forward rates implicit in active bond portfolio management.**
    Active bond managers' views on the future path of spot rates often differ from the path implied by the current forward curve. If they expect future spot rates to be lower than the forward rates, they will favor longer-maturity bonds.

* **Describe the strategy of rolling down the yield curve.**
    This strategy involves buying a bond with a maturity longer than the investment horizon. If the yield curve is upward-sloping and remains unchanged, the bond's price will increase as it "rolls down" the yield curve to a lower yield.

* **Explain the swap rate curve and why and how market participants use it in valuation.**
    The **swap rate curve** is a plot of the fixed rates on interest rate swaps for different maturities. It is often used as a benchmark for pricing and valuing fixed-income securities because it reflects the credit risk of commercial banks rather than the government, and it is available for a wider range of maturities.

* **Calculate and interpret the swap spread for a given maturity.**
    The **swap spread** is the difference between the swap rate and the yield on a government bond of the same maturity. It is a measure of the credit and liquidity risk of the interbank market.

* **Describe short-term interest rate spreads used to gauge economy-wide credit risk and liquidity risk.**
    Spreads such as the LIBOR-OIS spread are used as indicators of the health of the banking system. A widening of these spreads can signal increased credit risk and reduced liquidity.

* **Explain traditional theories of the term structure of interest rates and describe the implications of each theory for forward rates and the shape of the yield curve.**
    * **Unbiased Expectations Theory:** Forward rates are unbiased predictors of future spot rates. The shape of the yield curve depends on expectations of future interest rates.
    * **Liquidity Preference Theory:** Investors demand a liquidity premium for holding longer-maturity bonds. This causes the yield curve to be upward-sloping, on average.
    * **Market Segmentation Theory:** The supply and demand for bonds within different maturity segments determine the shape of the yield curve.

* **Explain how a bond's exposure to each of the factors driving the yield curve can be measured and how these exposures can be used to manage yield curve risks.**
    The yield curve is typically driven by three factors: level, steepness, and curvature. A bond's exposure to these factors can be measured using **key rate durations**. This allows portfolio managers to hedge against specific changes in the shape of the yield curve.

* **Explain the maturity structure of yield volatilities and their effect on price volatility.**
    Short-term interest rates are typically more volatile than long-term interest rates. However, the price volatility of a bond is a function of both yield volatility and duration, so long-term bonds usually have higher price volatility.

* **Explain how key economic factors are used to establish a view on benchmark rates, spreads, and yield curve changes.**
    Analysts use economic factors such as inflation, economic growth, and monetary policy to forecast the future direction of interest rates and yield spreads.

### The Arbitrage-Free Valuation Framework

This sub-topic introduces a more precise method for valuing bonds, especially those with complex features.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Explain what is meant by arbitrage-free valuation of a fixed-income instrument.**
    **Arbitrage-free valuation** means that the value of a bond is determined such that no arbitrage opportunities exist. This is achieved by discounting each of the bond's cash flows using the spot rate for that cash flow's maturity.

* **Calculate the arbitrage-free value of an option-free, fixed-rate coupon bond.**
    The arbitrage-free value is the sum of the present values of all future coupon and principal payments, where each payment is discounted by the corresponding spot rate.

* **Describe a binomial interest rate tree framework.**
    A **binomial interest rate tree** is a model that represents the possible future paths of the short-term interest rate. At each node in the tree, the interest rate can move up or down with a certain probability.

* **Describe the process of calibrating a binomial interest rate tree to match a specific term structure.**
    The interest rate tree is **calibrated** so that it produces arbitrage-free values for a set of benchmark bonds (usually on-the-run government bonds) that are consistent with their market prices.

* **Describe the backward induction valuation methodology and calculate the value of a fixed-income instrument given its cash flow at each node.**
    **Backward induction** is a process of valuing a bond by starting at the final maturity date and working backward to the present. At each node, the value of the bond is calculated as the present value of its future cash flows and expected future values.

* **Compare pricing using the zero-coupon yield curve with pricing using an arbitrage-free binomial lattice.**
    For an option-free bond, both methods will produce the same value. The binomial lattice is more powerful because it can be used to value bonds with embedded options.

* **Describe pathwise valuation in a binomial interest rate framework and calculate the value of a fixed-income instrument given its cash flows along each path.**
    **Pathwise valuation** involves calculating the present value of the bond's cash flows for each possible interest rate path in the tree and then taking the average of these present values.

* **Describe a Monte Carlo forward-rate simulation and its application.**
    **Monte Carlo simulation** can be used to generate a large number of random interest rate paths to value complex fixed-income securities, particularly those with path-dependent cash flows.

* **Describe term structure models and how they are used.**
    Term structure models (e.g., Vasicek model, Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model) are used to describe the evolution of the term structure of interest rates over time. They are used in the valuation and hedging of interest rate derivatives.

### Valuation and Analysis of Bonds with Embedded Options

This section extends the valuation framework to bonds that have embedded options, such as callable or putable bonds.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe fixed-income securities with embedded options.**
    * **Callable bond:** Gives the issuer the right to redeem the bond before maturity.
    * **Putable bond:** Gives the bondholder the right to sell the bond back to the issuer before maturity.
    * **Convertible bond:** Gives the bondholder the right to convert the bond into a specified number of shares of the issuer's common stock.

* **Explain the relationships between the values of a callable or putable bond, the underlying option-free (straight) bond, and the embedded option.**
    * Value of callable bond = Value of straight bond – Value of call option
    * Value of putable bond = Value of straight bond + Value of put option

* **Describe how the arbitrage-free framework can be used to value a bond with embedded options.**
    A binomial interest rate tree can be used to value bonds with embedded options by incorporating the exercise decision of the option at each node.

* **Explain how interest rate volatility affects the value of a callable or putable bond.**
    Higher interest rate volatility increases the value of both call and put options. Therefore, higher volatility decreases the value of a callable bond and increases the value of a putable bond.

* **Explain how changes in the level and shape of the yield curve affect the value of a callable or putable bond.**
    The value of embedded options is sensitive to the level and shape of the yield curve. For example, a callable bond is more likely to be called when interest rates are low.

* **Calculate the value of a callable or putable bond from an interest rate tree.**
    This is done using the backward induction method, checking at each node whether the embedded option would be exercised.

* **Explain the calculation and use of option-adjusted spreads.**
    The **option-adjusted spread (OAS)** is the constant spread that, when added to all the forward rates in a binomial tree, makes the arbitrage-free value of the bond equal to its market price. It is a measure of the bond's yield spread after adjusting for the value of the embedded option.

* **Explain how interest rate volatility affects option-adjusted spreads.**
    For a callable bond, a higher assumed volatility will lead to a lower calculated OAS. For a putable bond, a higher assumed volatility will lead to a higher calculated OAS.

* **Calculate and interpret effective duration of a callable or putable bond.**
    **Effective duration** is a measure of a bond's interest rate sensitivity that takes into account the effect of the embedded option. It is calculated by shocking the yield curve up and down and observing the change in the bond's price.

* **Compare effective durations of callable, putable, and straight bonds.**
    * A callable bond has a lower effective duration than a straight bond, especially when interest rates are low (negative convexity).
    * A putable bond has a lower effective duration than a straight bond, especially when interest rates are high.

* **Describe the use of one-sided durations and key rate durations to evaluate the interest rate sensitivity of bonds with embedded options.**
    **One-sided durations** (up-duration and down-duration) can be used to measure the interest rate sensitivity for rising and falling rates separately. **Key rate durations** measure the sensitivity to changes in specific points on the yield curve.

* **Compare effective convexities of callable, putable, and straight bonds.**
    A callable bond exhibits **negative convexity** when it is likely to be called, meaning its price appreciation is limited as rates fall. A putable bond exhibits positive convexity.

* **Calculate the value of a capped or floored floating-rate bond.**
    A **capped floater** has a maximum coupon rate, and a **floored floater** has a minimum coupon rate. They can be valued as a straight floating-rate bond plus or minus the value of the embedded cap or floor option.

* **Describe defining features of a convertible bond.**
    A **convertible bond** can be converted into a specified number of shares of the issuer's stock at the bondholder's option. It combines the features of a straight bond with an embedded call option on the stock.

* **Calculate and interpret the components of a convertible bond’s value.**
    The value of a convertible bond has two components: its value as a straight bond and the value of the conversion option.

* **Describe how a convertible bond is valued in an arbitrage-free framework.**
    Convertible bonds are typically valued using a model that incorporates both interest rate and stock price uncertainty, such as a two-factor binomial model.

* **Compare the risk–return characteristics of a convertible bond with the risk–return characteristics of a straight bond and of the underlying common stock.**
    A convertible bond offers the downside protection of a straight bond and the upside potential of the underlying stock. Its risk and return are intermediate between those of a straight bond and the stock.

**Equations:**

* **Value of convertible bond** = Value of straight bond + Value of call option on the issuer’s stock
* **Value of callable convertible bond** = Value of straight bond + Value of call option on the issuer’s stock – Value of issuer call option
* **Value of callable putable convertible bond** = Value of straight bond + Value of call option on the issuer’s stock – Value of issuer call option + Value of investor put option

### Credit Analysis Models

This sub-topic discusses models used to assess the credit risk of debt issuers.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Explain expected exposure, the loss given default, the probability of default, and the credit valuation adjustment.**
    * **Expected exposure:** The amount that could be lost in the event of a default.
    * **Loss given default (LGD):** The percentage of the exposure that is expected to be lost in a default.
    * **Probability of default (PD):** The likelihood that the issuer will default.
    * **Credit valuation adjustment (CVA):** An adjustment to the value of a derivative to account for the counterparty's credit risk.

* **Explain credit scores and credit ratings.**
    **Credit scores** (for individuals and small businesses) and **credit ratings** (for corporations and governments) are assessments of creditworthiness provided by rating agencies.

* **Calculate the expected return on a bond given transition in its credit rating.**
    The expected return on a bond can be calculated by considering the probability of the bond's credit rating changing and the expected price of the bond in each possible rating state.

* **Explain structural and reduced-form models of corporate credit risk, including assumptions, strengths, and weaknesses.**
    * **Structural models** (e.g., the Merton model) view default as occurring when the value of a company's assets falls below the value of its debt. They are based on the company's balance sheet structure.
    * **Reduced-form models** model the probability of default directly, without reference to the company's capital structure. They are more flexible but less intuitive than structural models.

* **Calculate the value of a bond and its credit spread, given assumptions about the credit risk parameters.**
    The value of a risky bond can be calculated by discounting its expected cash flows at the risk-free rate or by discounting its promised cash flows at a risk-adjusted discount rate. The credit spread is the difference between the yield on the risky bond and the yield on a comparable risk-free bond.

* **Interpret changes in a credit spread.**
    A widening credit spread indicates an increase in the perceived credit risk of the issuer. A narrowing spread indicates a decrease in credit risk.

* **Explain the determinants of the term structure of credit spreads and interpret a term structure of credit spreads.**
    The term structure of credit spreads shows the relationship between credit spreads and maturity. It can be upward-sloping, downward-sloping, or flat, depending on factors such as the economic cycle and market expectations.

* **Compare the credit analysis required for securitized debt to the credit analysis of corporate debt.**
    The credit analysis of **securitized debt** (e.g., mortgage-backed securities) focuses on the credit quality of the underlying collateral pool and the structure of the security (e.g., the priority of payments). The credit analysis of **corporate debt** focuses on the financial health and business prospects of the issuing company.

### Credit Default Swaps

This section covers credit default swaps (CDS), a widely used type of credit derivative.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe credit default swaps (CDS), single-name and index CDS, and the parameters that define a given CDS product.**
    A **CDS** is a derivative contract in which one party (the protection buyer) pays a periodic fee to another party (the protection seller) in exchange for a payment if a specified credit event occurs for a reference entity.
    * **Single-name CDS:** Covers a single reference entity.
    * **Index CDS:** Covers a portfolio of reference entities.

* **Describe credit events and settlement protocols with respect to CDS.**
    **Credit events** that can trigger a CDS payment include bankruptcy, failure to pay, and restructuring. Settlement can be either physical (the protection buyer delivers the defaulted bonds to the seller) or cash (the seller pays the buyer the difference between the par value and the recovery value of the bonds).

* **Explain the principles underlying and factors that influence the market’s pricing of CDS.**
    The price of a CDS (the CDS spread) is determined by the probability of default and the expected loss given default of the reference entity. It is also influenced by market liquidity and counterparty risk.

* **Describe the use of CDS to manage credit exposures and to express views regarding changes in the shape and/or level of the credit curve.**
    CDS can be used to hedge credit risk (by buying protection) or to take on credit risk (by selling protection). They can also be used in more complex strategies to speculate on changes in credit spreads or the shape of the credit curve.

* **Describe the use of CDS to take advantage of valuation disparities among separate markets, such as bonds, loans, equities, and equity-linked instruments.**
    Arbitrage opportunities can arise if the credit risk of a company is priced differently in the CDS market and the cash bond market (a "basis trade").

### Intercorporate Investments

This sub-topic covers the accounting and analysis of a company's investments in other companies.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe the classification, measurement, and disclosure under International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) for 1) investments in financial assets, 2) investments in associates, 3) joint ventures, 4) business combinations, and 5) special purpose and variable interest entities.**
    * **Investments in Financial Assets:** Classified based on the business model for managing the assets and their contractual cash flow characteristics. They are measured at either **amortized cost**, **fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI)**, or **fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL)**.
    * **Investments in Associates (Significant Influence, typically 20-50% ownership):** Accounted for using the **equity method**. The investment is initially recorded at cost and subsequently adjusted for the investor's share of the associate's profits or losses.
    * **Joint Ventures (Joint Control):** Typically accounted for using the **equity method**.
    * **Business Combinations (Control, typically >50% ownership):** Accounted for using the **acquisition method**. The acquirer recognizes the identifiable assets and liabilities of the acquiree at their fair values, and any excess of the purchase price is recorded as goodwill. The financial statements of the subsidiary are then **consolidated** with the parent.
    * **Special Purpose Entities (SPEs) and Variable Interest Entities (VIEs):** These are entities created for a specific purpose. An investor must consolidate an SPE/VIE if it is the primary beneficiary, meaning it has the power to direct the entity's activities and will absorb a significant portion of its expected losses or receive a significant portion of its expected returns.

* **Compare and contrast IFRS and US GAAP in their classification, measurement, and disclosure of investments in financial assets, investments in associates, joint ventures, business combinations, and special purpose and variable interest entities.**
    While broadly similar, some key differences exist. For example, US GAAP has stricter criteria for using the equity method. For investments in financial assets, IFRS uses a classification approach based on business model and cash flow characteristics, while US GAAP focuses more on the intent (e.g., held-to-maturity, available-for-sale, trading). The consolidation requirements for VIEs are also more detailed under US GAAP.

* **Analyze how different methods used to account for intercorporate investments affect financial statements and ratios.**
    * **FVTPL vs. FVOCI:** FVTPL recognizes all value changes in the income statement, increasing earnings volatility. FVOCI recognizes value changes in other comprehensive income, which bypasses the income statement, leading to smoother earnings but affecting book value.
    * **Equity Method:** Affects the investor's balance sheet (investment account) and income statement (share of associate's income). It can distort ratios like ROE and ROA.
    * **Consolidation:** The parent company's financial statements will include 100% of the subsidiary's assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses, which can significantly alter the parent's financial ratios and size.

### Employee Compensation: Post-Employment and Share-Based

This section discusses the accounting for various forms of employee compensation, focusing on pensions and stock options.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Contrast types of employee compensation.**
    Compensation can be current (salary, bonus) or deferred. Deferred compensation includes **pension plans** (defined benefit and defined contribution) and **share-based compensation** (stock options, restricted stock).

* **Explain how share-based compensation affects the financial statements.**
    When a company grants share-based compensation, it must recognize an expense on the income statement over the vesting period, based on the fair value of the award at the grant date. Changes in this net pension asset/liability are recognized in the income statement (as periodic pension cost) and in other comprehensive income.

* **Explain financial modeling and valuation considerations for post-employment benefits.**
    Analysts must scrutinize the actuarial assumptions (discount rate, expected return on assets, rate of compensation growth) used to calculate pension obligations, as these can significantly impact a company's financial position. ### Multinational Operations

This sub-topic covers the accounting for companies that operate in multiple currencies.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Describe and compare the functional currency and presentation currency.**
    * **Functional Currency:** The currency of the primary economic environment in which a foreign subsidiary operates. It's the currency in which the subsidiary generates and expends cash.
    * **Presentation Currency:** The currency in which the parent company presents its consolidated financial statements.

* **Describe the current rate and temporal methods of foreign currency translation.**
    The method used to translate a foreign subsidiary's financial statements depends on its functional currency.
    * **Current Rate Method:** Used when the subsidiary's functional currency is the local currency. All assets and liabilities are translated at the current exchange rate. Equity is translated at historical rates. The resulting translation adjustment is recognized in other comprehensive income.
    * **Temporal Method:** Used when the subsidiary's functional currency is the parent's presentation currency. Monetary assets and liabilities are translated at the current rate, while non-monetary items are translated at historical rates. The resulting translation gain or loss is recognized in the income statement.

* **Calculate the translation effects and evaluate the translation of a subsidiary’s balance sheet and income statement into the parent company’s presentation currency.**
    The choice of translation method can have a significant impact on the parent company's financial statements and ratios. The temporal method can lead to more volatile earnings due to the recognition of translation gains and losses in net income.

* **Explain how the effective tax rate of a multinational company is affected by its mix of profits and losses from different jurisdictions.**
    A multinational company's overall effective tax rate is a weighted average of the tax rates in the different countries where it operates. The company can manage its effective tax rate through its tax planning strategies, such as shifting income to low-tax jurisdictions.

## Economics

This topic covers macroeconomic concepts and their application to investment analysis, focusing on currency exchange rates and economic growth. 🌍

### Currency Exchange Rates: Understanding Equilibrium Value

This sub-topic explains the determination of currency exchange rates and the factors influencing their equilibrium value.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Calculate and interpret the bid-offer spread on a spot or forward currency quotation and describe the factors that affect the bid-offer spread.**
    The **bid-offer spread** is the difference between the price at which a dealer will buy a currency (the bid) and the price at which they will sell it (the offer). The spread is a transaction cost and is influenced by the currency's trading volume, volatility, and the time of day.

* **Identify a triangular arbitrage opportunity and calculate its profit, given the bid-offer quotations for three currencies.**
    **Triangular arbitrage** is a risk-free profit that can be earned if the cross-exchange rate implied by two currency pairs is different from the actual exchange rate quoted for that pair.

* **Explain spot and forward rates and calculate the forward premium/discount for a given currency.**
    * **Spot rate:** The exchange rate for immediate delivery.
    * **Forward rate:** The exchange rate for delivery at a future date.
    A currency is trading at a **forward premium** if its forward rate is higher than its spot rate, and at a **forward discount** if its forward rate is lower.

* **Calculate the mark-to-market value of a forward contract.**
    The mark-to-market value of a forward contract is the profit or loss that would be realized if the contract were closed out at the current forward rate.

* **Explain international parity conditions (covered and uncovered interest rate parity, forward rate parity, purchasing power parity, and the international Fisher effect).**
    These are theoretical relationships that link exchange rates, interest rates, and inflation.
    * **Covered Interest Rate Parity (CIRP):** The forward premium or discount is equal to the interest rate differential between two currencies. This relationship holds due to arbitrage.
    * **Uncovered Interest Rate Parity (UIP):** The expected change in the spot exchange rate is equal to the interest rate differential.
    * **Forward Rate Parity:** The forward exchange rate is an unbiased predictor of the future spot exchange rate.
    * **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP):** Exchange rates will adjust to equalize the price of a basket of goods in different countries.
    * **International Fisher Effect:** The interest rate differential between two countries is an unbiased predictor of the future change in the spot exchange rate.

* **Describe relations among the international parity conditions.**
    The parity conditions are interrelated. For example, if both UIP and CIRP hold, then forward rate parity must also hold.

* **Evaluate the use of the current spot rate, the forward rate, purchasing power parity, and uncovered interest parity to forecast future spot exchange rates.**
    In the short run, the current spot rate is often the best predictor of the future spot rate. Forward rates have not been shown to be reliable predictors. PPP is a long-run concept and is not useful for short-term forecasting. UIP generally does not hold in the short run.

* **Explain approaches to assessing the long-run fair value of an exchange rate.**
    Long-run fair value can be assessed using macroeconomic models that incorporate factors such as current account balances, purchasing power parity, and sustainable capital flows.

* **Describe the carry trade and its relation to uncovered interest rate parity and calculate the profit from a carry trade.**
    A **carry trade** involves borrowing in a low-interest-rate currency and investing in a high-interest-rate currency. The strategy is profitable as long as the high-yield currency does not depreciate by more than the interest rate differential. The carry trade is essentially a bet against UIP holding.

* **Explain how flows in the balance of payment accounts affect currency exchange rates.**
    A country with a persistent current account deficit must attract capital inflows to finance the deficit. This can put downward pressure on the currency.

* **Explain the potential effects of monetary and fiscal policy on exchange rates.**
    * **Expansionary monetary policy** (lower interest rates) tends to cause the currency to depreciate.
    * **Expansionary fiscal policy** (increased government spending) can have ambiguous effects, as it can lead to higher interest rates (appreciation) but also a larger current account deficit (depreciation).

* **Describe objectives of central bank or government intervention and capital controls and describe the effectiveness of intervention and capital controls.**
    Governments may intervene in the foreign exchange market to influence the value of their currency. **Capital controls** are restrictions on the flow of capital in and out of a country. The effectiveness of these measures is a subject of debate.

* **Describe warning signs of a currency crisis.**
    Warning signs of a currency crisis include a large current account deficit, a fixed exchange rate regime, a weak banking system, and rapid credit growth.

### Economic Growth

This section examines the drivers of economic growth and different theories of growth.

**Learning Outcomes:**

* **Compare factors favoring and limiting economic growth in developed and developing economies.**
    * **Developed economies:** Growth is driven by technological innovation and human capital.
    * **Developing economies:** Growth can be driven by capital accumulation, technology transfer, and institutional improvements. Factors limiting growth in developing economies often include a lack of physical and human capital, weak institutions, and political instability.

* **Describe the relation between the long-run rate of stock market appreciation and the sustainable growth rate of the economy.**
    In the long run, the growth rate of the stock market is linked to the growth rate of corporate earnings, which in turn is linked to the sustainable growth rate of the economy.

* **Explain why potential GDP and its growth rate matter for equity and fixed income investors.**
    **Potential GDP** is the maximum level of output that an economy can sustain without generating inflationary pressure. Its growth rate is a key determinant of the long-run growth rate of corporate earnings and the level of real interest rates.

* **Contrast capital deepening investment and technological progress and explain how each affects economic growth and labor productivity.**
    * **Capital deepening** is an increase in the capital-to-labor ratio. It leads to an increase in labor productivity but at a diminishing rate.
    * **Technological progress** is an improvement in the efficiency of production. It leads to a sustained increase in labor productivity and is the main driver of long-run economic growth.

* **Demonstrate forecasting potential GDP based on growth accounting relations.**
    The **growth accounting equation** breaks down the growth rate of potential GDP into the growth rate of technology, the growth rate of capital, and the growth rate of labor.

* **Explain how natural resources affect economic growth and evaluate the argument that limited availability of natural resources constrains economic growth.**
    While natural resources can be a source of wealth, countries that are heavily reliant on natural resources have often experienced lower long-run growth (the "resource curse"). The argument that limited natural resources will constrain growth has so far been proven wrong by technological progress, which has led to more efficient use of resources and the development of substitutes.

* **Explain how demographics, immigration, and labor force participation affect the rate and sustainability of economic growth.**
    The growth rate of the labor force is a key component of potential GDP growth. It is determined by population growth (which is affected by demographics and immigration) and the labor force participation rate.

* **Explain how investment in physical capital, human capital, and technological development affects economic growth.**
    Investment in physical capital (capital deepening), human capital (education and skills), and technological development (innovation) are all key drivers of economic growth.

* **Compare classical growth theory, neoclassical growth theory, and endogenous growth theory.**
    * **Classical growth theory (Malthus):** Predicts that population growth will outstrip the growth of resources, leading to a subsistence standard of living.
    * **Neoclassical growth theory (Solow):** Explains economic growth as a function of capital, labor, and technology. It predicts that economies will converge to a steady-state level of output per capita.
    * **Endogenous growth theory:** Argues that technological progress is not exogenous but is driven by investment in human capital and R&D. It predicts that there can be sustained long-run growth.

* **Explain and evaluate convergence hypotheses.**
    The convergence hypothesis suggests that poorer countries will grow faster than richer countries, leading to a convergence in per capita income levels. There is some evidence for "conditional convergence," meaning that countries with similar saving rates, population growth rates, and production functions will converge.

* **Describe the economic rationale for governments to provide incentives to private investment in technology and knowledge.**
    Investment in technology and knowledge has positive externalities (spillover benefits to the rest of the economy), so the private sector may underinvest in it. Government incentives, such as R&D tax credits and patents, can help to correct this market failure.

* **Describe the expected impact of removing trade barriers on capital investment and profits, employment and wages, and growth in the economies involved.**
    Removing trade barriers is generally expected to increase efficiency, promote competition, and lead to higher economic growth. It can also lead to short-term disruptions in employment and wages as resources are reallocated.

### **Step 1: Identify the Primary Duty**

First, determine which group is principally affected by your action. This will point you to the relevant Standard.

* Is it about your personal integrity, knowledge of the law, or professional reputation?
    * ➡️ Go to **Standard I: Professionalism**
* Is it about trading or using information in the market?
    * ➡️ Go to **Standard II: Integrity of Capital Markets**
* Is it about your relationship with or service to a client?
    * ➡️ Go to **Standard III: Duties to Clients**
* Is it about your relationship with your employer?
    * ➡️ Go to **Standard IV: Duties to Employers**
* Is it about the process of doing research or making recommendations?
    * ➡️ Go to **Standard V: Investment Analysis**
* Are there competing interests (yours vs. client, client vs. client)?
    * ➡️ Go to **Standard VI: Conflicts of Interest**
* Is it about the CFA designation itself?
    * ➡️ Go to **Standard VII: CFA Responsibilities**

---

### **Step 2: Apply the Specific Standard**

#### **Standard I: Professionalism**

* **(A) Knowledge of the Law:** Does the action violate the law, rules, or regulations? **Always follow the stricter rule** (your country's law vs. Code and Standards). Dissociate from any violation.
* **(B) Independence & Objectivity:** Could a gift, benefit, or compensation reasonably be expected to compromise your judgment?
    * **Violation:** An analyst accepts a weekend trip paid for by a company he covers.
    * **Not a Violation:** An analyst accepts a modest lunch from a company. The key is whether the gift is lavish or could influence decisions.
* **(C) Misrepresentation:** Are you knowingly stating something untrue or omitting a fact? This includes plagiarism, guaranteeing returns, or misstating credentials.
* **(D) Misconduct:** Does the action involve dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or otherwise reflect poorly on your professional reputation, integrity, or competence, even outside of work?

---

#### **Standard II: Integrity of Capital Markets**

* **(A) Material Nonpublic Information:** Do you have information that is **1) material** (its disclosure would impact a stock's price) AND **2) nonpublic**? If yes to both, you **cannot act or cause others to act** on it.
    * **Violation:** A CFO tells you at dinner that the company's earnings will miss estimates before the public announcement. You sell the stock.
    * **Not a Violation (Mosaic Theory):** You combine public information (supplier reports, industry trends, news articles) to conclude the company will miss earnings. You sell the stock.
* **(B) Market Manipulation:** Is the *intent* of your action to distort prices or trading volume to mislead the market?

---

#### **Standard III: Duties to Clients**

* **(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care:** Are you placing your client's interests before your own or your employer's? This is the core fiduciary duty.
* **(B) Fair Dealing:** Are you treating all clients fairly when disseminating information or taking action? (Fairly ≠ Equally).
    * **Violation:** Informing your top clients of a recommendation change before telling smaller clients.
    * **Not a Violation:** Releasing a report to all clients simultaneously, even if tech delays cause some to get it seconds later. Different service levels are okay if disclosed and available to all.
* **(C) Suitability:** Does your recommendation match the client's stated financial situation, objectives, and constraints (found in the Investment Policy Statement - IPS)? You MUST make this determination.
* **(D) Performance Presentation:** Is your performance reporting fair, accurate, and complete? No cherry-picking results or misstating history.
* **(E) Preservation of Confidentiality:** Are you keeping client information secret? **Exceptions:** disclosure of illegal activities, when required by law, or with client permission.

---

#### **Standard IV: Duties to Employers**

* **(A) Loyalty:** Are you undermining your employer's business? You must not harm your employer. Before leaving, you can make arrangements to compete, but you cannot solicit clients or take records while still employed.
    * **Violation:** While employed, using your work computer to create a business plan and contact lists for a new firm you are starting.
    * **Not a Violation:** Resigning and then, after your employment ends, using public information to contact former clients.
* **(B) Additional Compensation:** Are you accepting gifts or compensation that could create a conflict of interest with your employer's interest? You must get **written consent from all parties** involved.
* **(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors:** Do you have and enforce a compliance system to prevent and detect violations by those you supervise?

---

#### **Standard V: Investment Analysis, Recommendations, & Actions**

* **(A) Diligence & Reasonable Basis:** Is your analysis and recommendation supported by adequate research and investigation?
* **(B) Communication with Clients:** Are you clearly distinguishing between **facts and opinions**? Are you disclosing the general principles of your investment process?
* **(C) Record Retention:** Are you keeping the documents that support your analysis? Best practice is to retain records for **7 years**.

---

#### **Standard VI: Conflicts of Interest**

* **(A) Disclosure of Conflicts:** Do you have any personal or financial interest that could interfere with your duty to clients or your employer? You must make **full and fair disclosure**, delivered in plain language.
* **(B) Priority of Transactions:** Are you trading for clients before trading for your firm or yourself? The order is **clients first, then employer, then your personal account**.
* **(C) Referral Fees:** Are you disclosing any fee or benefit you receive for recommending a product or service to a client?

---

#### **Standard VII: Responsibilities as a CFA Member/Candidate**

* **(A) Conduct as Participants:** Are you cheating or otherwise compromising the integrity of the CFA Program?
* **(B) Reference to CFA Institute:** Are you using the designation correctly? It is an adjective, not a noun ("Jane Doe, CFA," not "Jane Doe is a CFA"). You cannot promise superior returns.
